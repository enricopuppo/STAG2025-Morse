\section{Experimental results}
\label{sec:results}

We consider a number of synthetic and real datasets, and we analyze them by using both homological persistence and scale-space.
We develop visualization idioms to illustrate the results obtained with the two methods and to highlight their differences.
Based on this analysis, we discuss the qualitative properties of features that are highlighted by the two methods. 

\subsection{Setup}
\label{sub:setup}

We consider 2D datasets encoded as regular grids of $R\times C$ values. 
The domain spanned by each dataset is first triangulated by joining each grid point $(i,j)$ with points $(i+1,j)$, $(i+1,j+1)$ to form one triangle, and $(i,j+1)$, $(i+1,j+1)$ to form another triangle. 
To avoid artifacts at the boundary, we extend our dataset with a dummy point, which is given a value larger than the maximum in the data range, and is connected with triangles to all boundary edges of the dataset, thus obtaining a mesh triangle mesh with the topology of a sphere, and a piecewise-linear function defined by the values associated with its vertices. 

The piecewise-linear function is processed with The Topology Toolkit \cite{ttk} to compute its persistent homology, and with the method described in \cite{Rocca:2013} to compute its scale-space. 
Both programs return a list of persistence pairs; for each pair:
\begin{itemize}
\item the position on the domain of the two critical points forming the pair: these are the points that give birth and kill a homological component in the case of persistent homology, or that annihilate together in the scale-space;
\item the types of the two critical points (either minimum--saddle, or saddle--maximum);
\item the values of the field at such two points;
\item the persistence or life of such a pair, in the case of persistent homology or scale-space, respectively. 
\end{itemize}

From these sequences, we produce several plots and diagrams, as described in the following.

 FINE TABELLA 



We consider the following synthetic and real datasets (see Table \ref{tab:data} for details):
\begin{itemize}
\item The \textbf{GausHills} datasets are stochastic terrains obtained by summing randomly distributed and scaled Gaussians of the type
\[A \mathrm{e}^{-\frac{(x-x_c)^2 + (y-y_c)^2}{2\sigma^2}}\]
where the center of the Gaussian $(x_c,y_c)$, its amplitude $A$ and its standard deviation $\sigma$ are random sampled from a given domain and intervals.
In \textbf{{GaussHills1}, just 6 Gaussians are sampled, the intervals used for $A$ and $\sigma$ are similar, and set so that the resulting function resembles a smooth landscape of mountains and pits. 
Dataset \textbf{{GaussHills2} is obtained by summing to\textbf{{GaussHills1} a larger number of Gaussians, where the intervals used for $A$ and $\sigma$ are smaller to add details at higher frequency.
Dataset \textbf{{GaussHills3} is obtained by adding more detail to \textbf{{GaussHills2} in a similar way.
The number of Gaussians added at each level is roughly the square of the number used at the previous level; the intervals for $A$ and $\sigma$ decrease about five times at each level. 
\item\textbf{{Wave} is a synthetic dataset representing a sinusoidal signal: in the $x$ direction, the frequency of the signal is constant, while its amplitude decreases linearly with $x$; in the $y$ direction, the amplitude of the signal is constant, while its frequency increases linearly with $y^2$. 
This dataset offers a wide yet controlled distribution of different amplitudes and frequencies. 
We also include two noisy versions of this dataset: in \textbf{Wave_normal_noise}, we add normal noise with a standard deviation that is $X\%$ of the range spanned by the dataset; in \textbf{Wave_spike_noise}, we add spikes randomly distributed over the domain, and having an intensity comparable to the data range.  
\item \textbf{Aletsch Glacier} is a section of the swisstopo DEM with a 25m horizontal resolution \cite{dhm25}, centered around Konkordiaplatz in the Aletsch glacier; it covers steep alpine areas full of dramatic drops and rises. 
\item \textbf{Cells}  is an image from the Low-frame-rate cell tracking dataset \cite{LFCT} captured at 10× magnification, depicting MCF10a cells expressing the red fluorescent protein mRuby. It features a large, almost flat, background region affected by noise, containing a relatively small number of islands corresponding to cells, with different variations of signal within different cells.
 \item \textbf{Mantle}, is a slice of the Earth Mantle Convection dataset \cite{scivis2021}, representing the temperature within the midmantle of the Earth approximately 500 million years ago. This dataset features a relatively smooth variation of the signal, exhibiting a more even distribution of maxima and minima, compared to natural and synthetic terrains, where minima are less frequent than maxima.  
\end{itemize}

\label{sub:signature}

Signature diagrams for homological persistence of the XXXX dataset. From the left: persistence diagram, aligned persistence diagram, persistence platforms.}

In persistent homology, the result of data analysis is most often subsumed by a \emph{persistence diagram}. 
It is a scatterplot, where each point represents a homological feature, corresponding to a pair of critical points that give birth and kill the feature in the filtration, respectively. 
Both axes of the scatterplot represent the \emph{range} of the function, and a point with coordinates $(x,y)$ represents a feature that was born at level $x$ and died at level $y$. 
The \emph{persistence} of the feature -- and its corresponding pair of critical points -- is the difference $y-x$. 
Since we always have $x\leq y$, all points lie above or on the diagonal; they are often connected to the diagonal with vertical segments to enhance the perception of persistence. 
See \ref{fig:p-diagrams} (left) for an example.

The persistence diagram represents explicitly the three relevant quantities that characterize each feature: the levels of birth and death, and its persistence. 
However, birth and death are not treated symmetrically, and persistence is not easy to read since the vertical bars rest on the diagonal.
To make persistence more evident, and \emph{aligned persistence diagram} can be used, which is a sheared version of the persistence diagram that brings the diagonal to the $x$ axis. 
In other words, each point $(x,y)$ represents a homological feature that was born at level $x$ and has a persistence $y$.
In this case, the $y$ axis represents persistence rather than the range, and the level of death of the feature is given by $x+y$.
See \ref{fig:p-diagrams} (center) for an example.
Note that the aligned persistence diagram doesn't show the death level explicitly. 

We introduce a third plot, the \emph{persistence platforms}, which has the same axes of the aligned persistence diagram (range vs persistence) and represents each feature with a orizontal segment from $(x_b,y)$ to $(x_d,y)$ where $x_b$ and $x_d$ are the levels of birth and death, respectively, and $y=x_d-x_b$ is the persistence.  
See \ref{fig:p-diagrams} (right) for an example.

In the case of scale-space, we cannot make an analogy of the persistence diagram, because the scale dimension is not homogeneous with the range. 
However, we can easily define diagrams that are similar to the aligned persistence diagram and persistence platforms.
In the \emph{aligned scale-space diagram}, the horizontal axis represents the function range (as in the aligned persistence diagram)  while the vertical axis represents scale (specifically, the standard deviation of the Gaussian filter). 
Each pair is represented with a point $(x,y)$ where $x$ is the level of the lowest point in the pair, while $y$ is the scale at which the pair annihilates.
Note that, in this case, the level of the highest point in the pair is not represented. 
Likewise, the \emph{scale-space platforms} plot has the same axes as the aligned scale-space diagram and represents each pair with a horizontal segment, having its left and right endpoints at the levels of the two critical points, and its $y$ coordinate at the scale at which the pair annihilates. 
See Figure \ref{fig:ss-diagrams} for an example.

In the following, we will disregard the classic persistence diagram, for which we have no comparison in the scale-space, and we will compare the two schemes by using the other four plots described above.

\subsection{Maps}
\label{sec:maps}
Signatures convey no information on the distribution of critical points, which form pairs defining topological features, on the domain of the function.
We complement them with maps that superimpose on the heatmap (greylevel) of the function the critical points, each represented with a bullet, colored according to its type (blue minimum, green saddle, and red maximum) and having a radius that is proportional to its persistence, or life in the scale-space, respectively. 

\subsection{Comparison plots}
\label{sub:comparison}
Our primary purpose is to visualize how differently the two approaches characterize topological features. 
All previous plots are well informative about each single analysis, but visual comparison of corresponding plots from the two methods requires some effort. 
We provide two more plots, each combining the two methods within the same plot, to enhance their differences. 
The \emph{comparison plot} shows one bullet per critical point of the functions. 
The two axes are persistence vs scale, and each point has coordinates $(x,y)$  where $x$ is its persistence and $y$ is its life in the scale-space. 
Note that, since the two methods may pair critical points differently, here we cannot represent pairs, but rather represent their constituent critical points, which are consistent for both methods.
To homogenize the two scales, we rescale all values in the range $[0,1]$.
\enrico{Aggiungere qui se facciamo gamma correction.} 
The bullets that fall close to the diagonal are points are classified similarly by the two methods; the more they are off the diagonal, the more different the two classifications. 

We pair this plot with a \emph{comparison map}, in which we represent each critical point, colored and scaled to show how off-diagonal it is. 
We compute the difference $y-x$, which is twice the signed distance from the diagonal, and we use this value to obtain a color from a divergent scale and its absolute value to set the radius of the bullet.
